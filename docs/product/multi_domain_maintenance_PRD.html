Status: Proposed PRD Product pivot · Data model · Terminology

Multi-Domain Maintenance — Assets & Templates

SquawkIt is a maintenance system that happens to know a lot about airplanes. This PRD generalizes it into a maintenance system for anything worth maintaining — a boat, a race car, a bicycle, a 3D printer, a shop compressor — without diluting what an aircraft owner gets today. The mechanism is a template chosen when you add a thing: it decides which spec fields exist, what components it has, which usage meters it accumulates, and what everything is called.

The thesis in one paragraph The squawk / task / log triad is domain-independent — something is wrong, something is due, something was done. That is the whole product, and it transfers unchanged. What does not transfer is the aircraft's shape: a fixed Airframe / Engine / Propeller component tree, three hardcoded hour counters, tail_number on the root message, and roughly 150 user-facing strings that say "aircraft," "tail number," "engine hours," or "AOG." Those four things become template-driven. Everything else stays exactly as it is.
Decisions this PRD asks for before Phase 1 Six choices gate the work: (1) does the Aircraft proto message and its Kotlin identifiers get renamed to Asset; (2) what the multi-asset home surface is called; (3) whether "Squawk" stays universal or becomes a per-template word; (4) whether the Core tier's 1-asset limit survives the pivot; (5) whether custom templates ship in v1; (6) whether an asset's template can be changed after creation. Recommendations are given inline and collected in §15.

1. Problem & Background

SquawkIt today is a general-aviation logbook: a fleet of aircraft, each with a maintenance log, a set of compliance tasks with due-status computation, and ad-hoc squawks. The architecture is local-first (SQLDelight EntityStore as the single source of truth, Firestore sync in the background), and the feature set — attachments, export, sharing, technicians, subscription — is mature.

The constraint is the market, not the software. General aviation in the US is on the order of 200k active aircraft; the addressable population of people who will pay a subscription to track annuals is small and already served by incumbents. Meanwhile the same person who logs a 100-hour inspection also has a boat that needs its impeller changed, a track car with a brake-fluid interval, a gravel bike whose chain is at 0.7% stretch, and a printer that has put 400 hours on a nozzle. They are tracking all of it in a notes app, a spreadsheet, or nothing.

Every one of those is the same job: a thing, a service schedule, a record of what was done, and a list of what's wrong with it right now. The app already does this well. It just insists that the thing is an airplane.

2. Goals & Non-Goals

Goals

  • Let a user add any maintainable thing — an aircraft, boat, car, motorcycle, bicycle, 3D printer, or generic shop equipment — and get a first-class squawk / task / log experience for it.
  • Introduce a template (chosen at creation) that determines the asset's spec fields, component tree, usage meters, terminology, and starter service schedule.
  • Generalize the data model so components are an arbitrary tree and usage is an arbitrary set of named meters — with no loss of any capability an aircraft has today.
  • Make terminology data, not code: one lexicon per template, resolved at render time, so an aviation user still reads "squawk," "tail number," and "AOG" while a printer owner reads "fault," "serial," and "down."
  • Ship starter task packs per template so a new asset arrives with a credible service schedule instead of an empty list — the single biggest onboarding lever in the pivot.
  • Guarantee that an existing aviation account sees a bit-identical experience after migration: same fields, same words, same due dates, same export layout.
  • Keep old and new clients interoperable through the sync engine for the entire mixed-version window.

Non-Goals

  • Parts inventory, purchasing, or cost tracking. Adjacent and tempting; a separate product decision.
  • Work orders, customer billing, or multi-tenant shop management. This is not a CMMS for a business; it is a maintenance record for an owner (and the people they trust with their stuff).
  • Telemetry / IoT ingestion (OBD-II, OctoPrint, NMEA, engine monitors). Meters are entered by a human in v1. The meter model is designed so automatic readings can be added later without a schema change.
  • A template marketplace or template sharing between users. Built-in catalog plus custom templates only.
  • Reworking Weight & Balance (still unbuilt) or Intelligent Search (still unbuilt) — both are noted for template-awareness when they are specified.
  • Localization of the lexicon. v1 lexicons are English. See §10 for the honest limitation this creates.

3. The Core Idea: Asset + Template

Two concepts replace one.

An Asset is the thing being maintained — the row in the home list, the owner of squawks, tasks, and logs. It is today's Aircraft, generalized: a name, a set of spec values, a component tree, and a set of meter readings.

A Template is the recipe that shapes an asset. It is not stored per asset; the asset holds a template_id and the template is resolved at read time. A template declares five things:

  1. Spec fields — what identity/detail fields the asset has, their labels, input types, and validation.
  2. Component slots — the default component tree (and whether the user may add to it).
  3. Meters — the usage counters this kind of thing accumulates, with units, precision, and a due-soon threshold.
  4. Lexicon — the words: what one of these is called, what a defect is called, what "down" is called.
  5. Starter tasks — an optional recommended service schedule, offered at creation.

The critical property: the template is a lens over a stable schema, never a schema of its own. Two assets with different templates are the same proto message and the same CollectionKind, stored in the same table, synced by the same engine. A template can be edited, versioned, or even deleted without any stored asset becoming unreadable — an unresolvable field key renders as a plain labeled value rather than disappearing. This is what keeps the local-first store and the sync engine untouched.

4. The Template Model

4.1 Spec fields

Every asset has a universal core — name, make, model, serial — because every template we can think of wants at least most of them (and "serial" degrades gracefully to blank). Everything beyond that is a template-declared field, stored as a key/value pair on the asset.

message SpecField {
  string key = 1;              // "tail_number", "vin", "hin", "frame_size"
  string label = 2;            // "Tail number", "VIN", "Hull ID (HIN)"
  InputType input = 3;         // TEXT | NUMBER | DATE | ENUM | YEAR
  bool required = 4;
  bool monospace = 5;          // render in JetBrains Mono — identifiers, serials
  string hint = 6;             // "N12345"
  repeated string enum_values = 7;
  string group = 8;            // section header on the edit form
}

Values live on the asset as strings with typed parsing at read. String storage is deliberate: it makes an unknown-to-this-client field round-trip losslessly through an older or newer app version, which matters because two devices on the same account can be on different releases.

4.2 Component tree

The fixed Aircraft → repeated Engine → Propeller → (hub, blades) shape becomes a recursive tree:

message Component {
  string id = 1;               // stable UUID, generated on creation — the join key
  string slot_key = 2;         // "airframe" | "engine" | "propeller" | "hotend" | "hull"
  string label = 3;            // user-visible; defaults from the template slot
  string make = 4;
  string model = 5;
  string serial = 6;
  repeated Component children = 7;
  repeated SpecValue spec = 8; // slot-specific extras (e.g. blade count, nozzle diameter)
}

The template declares the default slots and their cardinality:

message ComponentSlot {
  string key = 1;
  string label = 2;            // "Engine", "Hotend", "Drivetrain"
  Cardinality cardinality = 3; // EXACTLY_ONE | ZERO_OR_ONE | ZERO_OR_MORE
  bool requires_serial = 4;
  repeated ComponentSlot children = 5;
  repeated string meter_keys = 6;  // meters that accrue against this component
}
Why component IDs, not serials Today a log, task, or squawk points at a component with ComponentType + component_serial. That breaks the moment two components share a blank serial (common on bikes and printers) and it re-points silently when a component is replaced with a new serial. A stable component_id fixes both: replacing an engine creates a new component and the old logs stay attached to the old one, which is exactly the history an owner wants. Legacy fields stay dual-written for the aircraft template through the compatibility window (§8).

4.3 Meters

The three hardcoded hour doubles become a declared set of counters. This is the single highest-leverage generalization: it is what makes "every 5,000 miles," "every 500 print hours," and "every 100 tach hours" the same feature.

message Meter {
  string key = 1;              // "airframe_hours", "odometer", "print_hours", "filament_kg"
  string label = 2;            // "Airframe time", "Odometer", "Print hours"
  string unit = 3;             // "hrs", "mi", "km", "kg", "cycles", "nm"
  int32 decimals = 4;          // 1 for tach, 0 for odometer, 2 for filament
  double due_soon_threshold = 5; // 10 hrs · 500 mi · 25 print hrs — replaces the +10f constant
  bool monotonic = 6;          // true for odometers/tach: readings may not decrease
  bool per_component = 7;      // engine hours are per-engine; airframe time is per-asset
}

A log entry carries repeated MeterReading { string meter_key; string component_id; double value; }. MaintenanceOverview's three current_*_time fields become repeated MeterReading current, computed the same way (max over logs).

EngineHourRule { interval_hours } generalizes to MeterRule { string meter_key; double interval; }, and the task stops inferring its metric from component type — it names the meter. Multiple rules on one task keep today's semantics (whichever comes first), which now naturally supports "every 6 months or 5,000 miles, whichever comes first" with no new machinery.

4.4 Lexicon

The terminology layer. One lexicon per template, resolved into a CompositionLocal at the asset scope.

message Noun { string singular = 1; string plural = 2; string article = 3; }  // "a" / "an"

message Lexicon {
  Noun asset = 1;        // aircraft · boat · car · bike · printer · machine
  Noun defect = 2;       // squawk · issue · fault
  Noun task = 3;         // inspection · service · maintenance
  Noun log = 4;          // logbook entry · service record · maintenance entry
  Noun component = 5;    // component · part · system
  Noun technician = 6;   // mechanic · technician · shop
  string ready_status = 7;      // "Airworthy" · "Ready" · "In service"
  string down_status = 8;       // "AOG" · "Dead in the water" · "Down"
  string down_status_long = 9;  // "Aircraft on ground" · "Out of service"
  string home_label = 10;       // "Fleet" · "Garage" · "Workshop"
  string compliance_mandatory = 11;  // "Airworthiness directive" · "Safety recall"
  string compliance_advisory = 12;   // "Service bulletin" · "TSB" · "Manufacturer notice"
  string authority_label = 13;       // "FAA" · "NHTSA" · "Manufacturer"
}

4.5 Starter task packs

A template may ship a list of recommended tasks. At asset creation the user is offered them as a single opt-in step ("Add 6 recommended services?") with per-item checkboxes, and can add the pack later from an empty Tasks tab. They are created as ordinary MaintenanceTask rows — editable, deletable, in no way special after creation.

This is where the pivot earns its keep. An aircraft owner already knows their annual is due every 12 months. A person who just bought a used boat does not know the impeller is annual and the zincs are semi-annual, and an app that tells them is worth paying for. Starter packs are also the cheapest possible content moat: they are data, not code.

Liability posture on starter packs Packs are labeled recommendations, not authority, with a one-line disclaimer on the offer step pointing the user to their manufacturer's service manual. For the aviation template specifically, the pack contains only intervals that are regulatory-universal (annual, 100-hour, ELT, transponder, pitot-static) — never aircraft-model-specific intervals, and never an AD or SB, which must remain user-entered.

4.7 Built-in vs. custom templates

Built-in templates are compiled into the binary and versioned with the app. They are not user data, do not sync, and cannot be corrupted by a bad sync. Custom templates are user data: a new CollectionKind.AssetTemplate (wireName "asset_template"), synced like any other entity, hydrated at sign-in as a top-level kind.

Template resolution is by template_id with a built-in-wins-then-custom lookup and a lenient fallback: if the id resolves to nothing (custom template deleted, or an id written by a newer app version), the asset renders with the generic equipment lexicon and every stored spec value shown under its raw key as a labeled row. Data is never hidden and never dropped.

Recommendation
Ship the seven built-ins in v1 and defer custom templates to v1.1. The built-in catalog covers the stated use cases, and a custom-template editor is a substantial UI surface (field builder, slot builder, meter builder) that would gate the pivot on its own timeline. The AssetTemplate proto and CollectionKind ship in v1 so the storage shape is settled; only the editor is deferred.

5. Data Model Changes

All changes are additive at the proto level. No field is removed or renumbered in v1; legacy fields are dual-written and deprecated in place.

6. Due-Status Generalization

TaskDueManagerImpl keeps its structure and loses three aviation assumptions:

  1. Metric selection. Today: component == AIRFRAME ? airframe_time : engine_hour. New: each MeterRule names its meter; the current value is the max reading for that (meter_key, component_id) pair across the asset's logs. A task with no meter rule is date-only, as today.
  2. Due-soon thresholds. Today: hardcoded 1 month and +10f. New: the date threshold stays 1 month (domain-independent and well-tuned); the meter threshold comes from Meter.due_soon_threshold. This alone is the difference between "due soon at 10 miles remaining" and a usable car experience.
  3. Force-due. force_due_engine_hour becomes force_due_meter { key, value }, with the legacy float mapped onto the engine-hours meter on read.

Everything else — force-complied, linked rules with cycle protection, one-time tasks moving to history, on-condition, immediate — is meter-agnostic already and is not touched.

Regression bar for this section The existing TaskDueManager test suite must pass unmodified against the generalized implementation, with the aircraft template supplying the meters. If a test needs editing to pass, the generalization changed aviation behavior and the change is wrong. This is the cheapest available proof that the pivot is non-destructive.

7. UX Changes, Surface by Surface

7.1 Creating an asset

Today: Add aircraft → a form with airframe and engine sections. New: a three-step flow, the first step of which is the whole pivot.

Step 1 — Pick a template"What are you taking care of?"
Icon grid: Aircraft · Boat · Car · Motorcycle · Bicycle · 3D printer · Equipment · Custom
Step 2 — IdentityName + template spec fields, template-labeled
Step 3 — ComponentsPre-filled slots; required-serial slots flagged; add/remove where cardinality allows
Step 4 — Starter servicesOptional, per-item checkboxes, "Skip" is a first-class button

Steps 3 and 4 are skippable; an asset with a name and a template is valid. This matters — the current aircraft form's serial-number validation is appropriate for an aircraft and hostile for a bike whose owner has never looked at the frame number.

Homogeneous-account shortcut: if the account already has assets and they are all one template, step 1 pre-selects that template with a "change" affordance. An aviation user adding their second aircraft sees essentially today's flow with one extra tap.

7.2 The home surface

Today the fleet list is homogeneous. It becomes a mixed-type list where each row carries a small template icon and its status line uses that asset's lexicon ("2 open squawks · AOG" next to "1 open issue · Due in 300 mi"). Grouping by template is offered once the account holds more than one template type.

Decision needed — home surface name
"Fleet" is wrong for a bike and a printer. Candidates: Workshop, Garage, Shop, Hangar, Things. Recommendation: "Workshop" as the neutral default, with the label overridden by Lexicon.home_label when every asset in the account shares one template — so an all-aircraft account still reads "Fleet" and an all-car account reads "Garage." "Shop" reads commercial; "Things" is too casual for the brand's precision register.

7.3 Asset overview

The four-tab layout (Overview → Squawks → Tasks → Logs) is preserved exactly; only labels change per lexicon. The Overview tab's ConfigurationCard renders the component tree generically instead of the hardcoded airframe/engine/prop sections, and AogAlertSection becomes a down-status alert section keyed on Lexicon.down_status.

7.4 Log entry

The smart component picker already loads real components and auto-fills serials — that behavior is preserved and improved, since it now walks the component tree rather than three special cases. The three hour fields are replaced by one field per applicable meter, labeled and unit-suffixed from the meter definition, shown only for meters relevant to the selected component.

7.5 Export

The paper-logbook tab layout becomes the aircraft template's layout. Other templates get a generic layout whose columns are derived from the lexicon and the meter set. Sheet names, file names, and the README are lexicon-driven. The pipeline, ZIP structure, delivery, and history are untouched.

7.6 Settings, sharing, technicians

Label-only changes. The technician record's certificate fields become conditionally shown based on a lexicon flag, so a bike or printer owner doesn't see "Certificate number / A&P / IA."

8. Migration & Version Compatibility

This is the highest-risk section. Two devices on one account can run different app versions and sync the same documents, so a migration that is correct locally can still corrupt data through the sync engine.

8.1 Backfill

On first launch of the new version, a local migration runs over every stored Aircraft:

  • template_id = "aircraft", template_version = shipped version.
  • name = tail_number if non-empty, else "$make $model".
  • spec["tail_number"] = tail_number.
  • Component tree built from the legacy fields: one airframe component carrying the asset's own make/model/serial; one engine component per Engine; a propeller child per engine with hub and blade children. IDs are generated deterministically from (asset_id, slot_key, index) so the same IDs are produced on every device independently — no ID reconciliation over sync.
  • Logs, tasks, and squawks get component_id resolved from (component_type, component_serial); an unmatched serial leaves component_id empty and the row falls back to legacy display. It is never dropped.
  • Log meter readings backfilled: airframe_time → airframe_hours, engine_hour → engine_hours, prop_time → prop_hours.

The backfill is idempotent and non-dirtying: it does not mark rows dirty and does not push. Migrated fields are written on the next legitimate edit. This avoids a full-account re-push on upgrade day and keeps the mixed-version window quiet.

Deterministic IDs are load-bearing If component IDs were random per device, the same aircraft would migrate to different component IDs on a phone and a tablet, and last-writer-wins would silently reassign every log's component. Deriving IDs from (asset_id, slot_key, index) makes the migration a pure function of data that already synced. This must be covered by an explicit test that migrates the same payload twice and asserts identical output.

8.2 Mixed-version behavior

ScenarioBehavior
Old client reads an aircraft-template asset written by a new clientCorrect. Legacy fields are dual-written; new fields are ignored by Wire.
Old client reads a boat written by a new clientDegraded but safe: it shows an aircraft with the boat's make/model/serial, no tail number, no engines. It must not crash and must not delete anything. Guarded by the version floor below.
Old client edits a boatThe real risk. An old client writing an aircraft-shaped payload could drop components and spec — Wire's unknown-field retention normally preserves them, and this must be verified with a test, not assumed.

Version floor. Non-aircraft templates are gated on a minimum client version recorded on the account. Until every signed-in device reports at or above the floor, the create-asset flow offers only the aircraft template and shows "Update your other devices to add other kinds of equipment." This is a soft, self-clearing gate — anonymous and single-device accounts never see it — and it retires with the dual-write in Phase 5.

9. How Terminology Actually Works

820 strings across 25 strings.xml files; roughly 150 name the domain. They fall into three buckets:

BucketCount (approx.)Treatment
Domain-neutral — "Save", "Delete", "Due soon", "Open", "Dismissed"~670Untouched.
Noun-substitutable — "Add aircraft", "No aircraft yet", "Delete this squawk?"~110Become format strings: "Add %1$s" resolved against the lexicon at render.
Structurally aviation — "Airworthiness directive", "Tail number", "Aircraft on ground", "Tach time"~40Move into the lexicon or the template's field/meter labels as whole strings, not substitutions.

Mechanically: a LocalLexicon CompositionLocal is provided at the asset scope (and at a sensible default for account-level screens), plus a small formatter that handles sentence-case, title-case, plural, and indefinite article. Strings stay in strings.xml — the repo's hard rule is unchanged; they simply gain a placeholder.

Honest limitation: noun substitution does not localize "Add %1$s" + a noun works in English and breaks in languages with grammatical case or gender agreement (German dative, Polish accusative, French gender-dependent articles). The app is English-only today, so this is not a regression — but it is a ceiling we are choosing. When localization lands, the ~110 substitutable strings will need either per-locale noun forms with case variants, or whole-sentence variants keyed by template. The lexicon proto is structured to carry per-locale noun forms later; nothing here forecloses the fix, and nothing here does it.

What the aviation user sees: nothing. The aircraft lexicon reproduces every current string exactly, and a snapshot test asserts that rendering the aviation lexicon produces byte-identical strings to the current resources. Terminology drift on the existing product would be the most damaging possible outcome of this work, so it is tested, not reviewed.

10. Brand & Naming

The app name stays SquawkIt. "Squawk" is aviation slang, but "squawk it" already reads as a verb — report the thing that's wrong — to someone who has never flown. Renaming the app would cost the store listing, the ASO history, the domain, and a second identifier migration on a codebase still carrying wingslog from the first one. The brand is an asset; the jargon inside the product is what needs to flex.

Decision needed — is "squawk" universal or per-template?
Recommendation: per-template, with "squawk" retained for aviation. The lexicon already carries defect, so this costs nothing structurally. A boat owner reading "3 open squawks" learns a word they didn't ask to learn; the brand still says SquawkIt on the icon, which is where a brand belongs. The counter-argument — that a single universal verb is stronger branding — is real, and if it wins, the change is one lexicon field set identically across templates.

Visual identity is unchanged. The aviation palette (Aviation Blue, Instrument Amber ≤10%, semantic forest/amber status), Space Grotesk titles, and JetBrains Mono for identifiers read as precision instrument, not airplane. That register is exactly right for a person servicing a printer or a race car. Dynamic color stays off. PRODUCT.md and DESIGN.md need one revision each: the "Users" section broadens beyond aircraft owners and mechanics, and the reference to "tail numbers, serials, tach times" as the monospace set becomes "identifiers, serials, and meter readings."

Store positioning is a genuine tension: one listing must sell to a pilot and a cyclist. Recommendation is to lead with the universal promise ("Maintain anything with the rigor of an aircraft logbook") and use the screenshot set to show breadth, keeping aviation as the credibility proof rather than the headline. This deserves its own treatment before launch and is out of scope here.

11. Subscription & Entitlement Impact

Mechanically trivial: SubscriptionManager.aircraftLimit() → assetLimit(), FREE_AIRCRAFT_LIMIT → FREE_ASSET_LIMIT, and the four upsell strings drop the word "aircraft" in favor of the neutral asset noun. Attachments, email export, and sharing gates are unaffected.

Decision needed — does Core stay at one asset?
A one-aircraft free tier is coherent: most GA owners own one airplane, so free is a complete product and the paywall triggers on genuine fleet management. In a multi-domain world the same limit reads differently — a person with a bike and a printer hits the wall on day one, before the app has proven anything. Recommendation: raise Core to 3 assets and lean harder on attachments, email export, and sharing as the Heavy differentiators. It trades some immediate conversion pressure for a far better first week, and the multi-asset habit is what makes Heavy worth buying later. This is a revenue decision, not an engineering one, and should be made with whatever conversion data exists rather than from this document.
